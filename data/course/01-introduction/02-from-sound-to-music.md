Is music a human product or is it innate in nature? Both birds and people sing beautifully, so it's a lovely doubt to live with. Fact is, that music is a form of sound.

## What is sound

The technical standard established by ANSI/ASA S1.1‑2013 defines it as:

> "(a) Oscillation in pressure, stress, particle displacement, particle velocity, etc., propagated in a medium with internal forces (e.g., elastic or viscous), or the superposition of such propagated oscillation.
>
> (b) Auditory sensation evoked by the oscillation described in (a)."

The medium is usually air (and the thin walls that separate you from your neighbors). Sound travels as a mechanical wave through it. Sound waves are generated by a sound source, such as a vibrating diaphragm of a loudspeaker. As the sound source vibrates the surrounding medium, mechanical disturbances propagate away from the source at the local speed of sound, thus resulting in a sound wave.

Waves oscillate with a certain **frequency** that is measured in **hertz** (abbr. Hz).

Not all vibrations can be perceived by the human ear: just frequencies between 20 and 20 KHz.
At the lower end of the spectrum there are unsurprisingly the _low frequencies_, and at the other end the _high frequencies_. What you hear on the phone when calling your aunt is between 300 and 3400 Hz. The A note above the middle C of the piano has nowadays a frequency of 440 Hz. Your dog, your bat or your dolphin can ear way higher frequencies than us.

## The octave relationship

The term frequency surely got some traction after the advent of telecommunication. Radio stations operated at specific frequencies, good mates said to be on the same frequency. Lately people say that they _vibe_ together after all. In music and psychoacoustics we accept the (vibration) frequency instead of the sound itself metonymy.

To associate any audible event to a single number is reductive. Sound has a lot of characteristics that are combination of various parameters that evolve over its duration.

There are sounds that can be well described with a single number though: **pure tones**. A pure tone is the sound generated by a single sinusoidal wave. Listen to the following examples:

The second one sounds distinctively higher than the first, but they feel very similar, much similar than the previous example isn't it? We say that the second sound is **one octave higher than the first**. The name _octave_ is a retronym whose etymology will be discussed in the scales module, but if you play or sing you have surely been exposed to the concept.

### Discovery of the octave

Ancient populations came to similar conclusions and invented devices that produced sound that they found pleasant to the hear, like [the monochord][wiki:monochord]:

![Two marine trumpets. They are not ancient monochords but the action principle is the same, a single string tied over a board.](~/src/assets/pictures/monochords.jpg?width=400)

They were carelessly plucking their monochords while sitting on their porches when they noticed that stopping the string at specific points produced other cool sounds.

Plucking the string while stopping it **exactly at its half** produced a sound very similar to that obtained by plucking the string freely. While distinctively higher in pitch, playing the two sounds together reinforced the idea that they were sharing the same essence.

The early evolution of music didn't happen in single milestone events but rather over independent experiences that led to similar observations.
